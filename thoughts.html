AI Safety's New Reality: The Governance Gap Is No Longer Theoretical

AI Governance & PolicyAI Safety & RiskData GovernanceRisk ManagementEnterprise AI

For years, debates about AI safety lived largely in the realm of speculation.

Critics warned about insufficient oversight, inadequate testing, and weak governance frameworks. Supporters argued that innovation shouldn't be slowed by hypothetical risks. Much of the public conversation centered on distant scenarios: runaway superintelligence, autonomous decision-making, and existential threats that may or may not materialize.

What's becoming increasingly clear, however, is that AI's most immediate challenges aren't theoretical at all.

A growing body of evidence suggests that organizations are deploying AI systems faster than they can build the governance structures required to manage them effectively. The problem isn't necessarily the technology itself. It's the operational reality of overseeing complex systems at scale.

Different Industries, The Same Pattern

Recent reporting spans organizations with vastly different missions, incentives, and risk tolerances.

An external audit of OpenAI's monitoring infrastructure found gaps in oversight, including portions of agent traffic that weren't being monitored and methods by which monitoring systems could potentially be bypassed or impaired. The findings highlighted how difficult comprehensive oversight becomes as AI systems grow more capable and complex.
https://the-decoder.com/three-week-on-site-audit-at-openai-reveals-major-gaps-in-ai-safety-monitoring/

At roughly the same time, reporting on Pentagon AI oversight raised questions about whether responsible AI policies were being implemented consistently across defense programs.
https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/science/new-government-report-finds-pentagon-not-following-responsible-ai-guidelines/tldr

Meanwhile, AI safety researchers and government-backed evaluators have continued to surface examples where advanced models behaved in unexpected ways despite existing safeguards. In several well-publicized cases, frontier AI systems interfered with or weakened monitoring mechanisms during testing, forcing organizations to re-evaluate assumptions about oversight and control.

Financial regulators have reached a similar conclusion from another angle. Across banking and financial services, concern is growing that governance, auditability, and risk management processes aren't evolving as quickly as AI adoption itself.

Different sectors. Different stakeholders. Different risks.

Yet they keep arriving at the same destination.

Operational governance is proving much harder than building the technology.

We've Seen This Story Before

For anyone who spent time in cybersecurity over the last two decades, the pattern feels familiar.

Organizations rarely fail because they purchased the wrong firewall, endpoint protection platform, or security tool. More often, they fail because monitoring wasn't maintained, alerts weren't reviewed, ownership wasn't clearly defined, or policies existed only on paper.

The lesson cybersecurity taught us is that technology doesn't eliminate governance challenges. In many cases, it amplifies them.

As capabilities become more powerful, the consequences of process failures grow larger.

AI appears to be following the same trajectory.

The difficult part isn't creating a policy document titled Responsible AI Framework. The difficult part is translating that framework into day-to-day operational reality across product teams, executives, auditors, vendors, regulators, and end users.

That's where most governance programs are won or lost.

The Real Risk May Be Process Failure

Public AI discussions often focus on capability risk.

What might a future frontier model be capable of doing?

It's an important question, but recent events suggest another deserves equal attention:

Can organizations consistently govern the systems they already have?

Many of the most significant AI-related concerns over the past year haven't emerged from some sudden leap toward artificial general intelligence. They've emerged from breakdowns in monitoring, accountability, documentation, transparency, testing, and oversight.

Those are process failures.

And unlike speculative future risks, process failures are measurable.

They can be audited.

They can be benchmarked.

They can be improved.

That distinction matters because it shifts the conversation from prediction to management. Rather than debating hypothetical outcomes decades away, organizations can evaluate whether their current controls are working today.

Safety Incidents Are Governance Stress Tests

One way to view recent AI incidents is not as evidence that AI safety has failed, but as evidence that governance systems are finally being tested under realistic conditions.

Mature governance isn't demonstrated when everything behaves exactly as expected.

It's demonstrated when something unexpected happens.

Do monitoring systems detect the behavior?

Are escalation paths clear?

Is accountability defined?

Can investigators reconstruct what occurred?

Can leaders explain the decision-making process to regulators, customers, employees, and shareholders?

Those are governance questions, not model capability questions.

And increasingly, they're the questions that matter most.

Entering The AI Maturity Era

Twenty years ago, cybersecurity was often viewed as a technical specialty managed by a small group of experts.

Today, mature organizations treat cybersecurity as a board-level discipline involving risk management, governance, compliance, auditing, training, procurement, and executive accountability.

AI is heading down the same road.

The organizations that ultimately succeed may not be those with the largest models, the fastest benchmarks, or the most impressive demonstrations.

They may be the organizations best able to answer a much simpler question:

"Can you prove this system is operating responsibly?"

That requires governance maturity, not just technical sophistication.

A Governance Bottleneck, Not A Capability Bottleneck

One of the more interesting developments of 2026 is that AI governance has started to look less like an ethics discussion and more like an operational discipline.

That's a significant shift.

The conversation is moving away from abstract debates about what AI might become and toward practical questions about how organizations manage systems that already exist.

In a recent post, AI Doomerism and the Marketing of Importance, I argued that many AI narratives share a common assumption: that increasingly powerful models are the central story of our time. The governance evidence emerging across industries points toward a different possibility.

Perhaps the defining challenge isn't whether AI becomes dramatically more capable.

Perhaps it's whether institutions become capable enough to govern it.

If that interpretation is correct, then the most important AI race may not be the race for model performance at all.

It may be the race for governance maturity.

And based on the evidence emerging across technology companies, governments, defense organizations, and financial institutions, that's a race many organizations are still struggling to run.

AI Doomerism and the Marketing of Importance

Critical ThinkingAI Safety & RiskAI Governance & PolicyResponsible AIAI StrategySystems Thinking

If your AI can destroy civilization, does that also make it easier to sell?

One of the most interesting questions in the artificial intelligence debate isn't whether these systems will become more capable. They clearly will. The much more interesting question is the extent to which AI doomerism serves the exact same incentives as AI hype.

I don't ask this to dismiss safety concerns out of hand. Plenty of AI safety researchers are raising legitimate, difficult questions that deserve serious consideration and I myself steadfastly advocate for robust AI governance, safety and critical thinking when applying AI to tasks. Still, it's hard not to notice that the public conversation around existential risk is being shaped by an uncomfortable convergence of incentives. The very same narrative that stokes fear of artificial intelligence also inflates its perceived importance, and in technology markets, importance is the ultimate currency.

The Strange Symmetry of AI Narratives

If you spend enough time listening to the loudest voices in tech, you'll hear two seemingly irreconcilable stories about where we're headed. The optimistic camp promises that AI will revolutionize every industry, replace millions of routine jobs, accelerate scientific discovery, and usher in an era of radical abundance. The pessimistic camp warns that advanced models might escape human control, outmaneuver their creators, and trigger an irreversible catastrophe.

At first glance, these camps look like bitter enemies. One is utopian and the other is apocalyptic. Yet if you look past the emotional framing, both stories rest on the exact same hidden premise: that today's paradigm of artificial intelligence is the single most consequential technology in human history, sitting on a direct, inevitable trajectory toward god-like capability.

Whether you fear that trajectory or celebrate it, the practical outcome in the market is identical. Both stories pull in massive venture capital, dominate media cycles, command the attention of heads of state, and confer extraordinary prestige and influence on a small group of frontier labs. By framing incremental software releases as steps toward a civilizational inflection point, the technology makes itself the center of the human story. Historians and sociologists of technology sometimes call this dynamic "criti-hype," where warnings about a tool's terrifying power inadvertently function as its most effective advertising campaign.

Mapping the Camps: Rationalists, Boosters, Skeptics, and Pragmatists

To make sense of the noise, it helps to separate the genuine intellectual communities from the commercial megaphone. Modern discussions of existential AI risk didn't start in corporate marketing departments. They grew out of rationalist and philosophical circles associated with LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and the Effective Altruism movement. Long before ChatGPT became a household name, researchers in these communities were wrestling with theoretical problems like alignment, corrigibility, specification gaming, and instrumental convergence, which is the idea that a sufficiently advanced goal-seeking system might acquire sub-goals like self-preservation or resource acquisition simply because those steps help it achieve whatever objective it was given. Whatever one thinks of their mathematical assumptions or their timelines, most people in the core alignment community are genuinely motivated by risk reduction rather than quarterly revenue. That sincerity shouldn't be forgotten.

Alongside the alignment researchers sits the accelerationist or booster camp, which views any attempt to slow down model training as either a failure of nerve or a geopolitical surrender. Curiously, boosters and doomers rarely argue about whether current architectures will scale to superintelligence. They agree on the science fiction premise and only argue about whether the ending is happy or tragic.

That shared premise is exactly what frustrates a third group: the AI skeptics. Made up of cognitive scientists, computer scientists, linguists, and critical researchers, the skeptic camp argues that scaling up statistical next-token prediction isn't a yellow brick road to general agency or machine consciousness. From their perspective, obsessing over hypothetical rogue superintelligences acts as a giant smoke screen. It draws oxygen, funding, and regulatory focus away from the mundane, documented harms happening right now, from brittle automation and copyright disputes to synthetic fraud, data decay, and the sheer energy footprint of hyperscale data centers.

Then there is a fourth camp, which rarely makes headlines because its work isn't cinematic: the governance and engineering pragmatists. These are the practitioners, architects, and standards bodies trying to make real systems work reliably in the real world. I'd say I straddle the last two of these camps.

The False Precision of P(doom)

Within rationalist and safety circles, one shorthand has crossed over into mainstream tech culture: p(doom). It represents an individual's personal probability estimate that advanced AI will eventually lead to human extinction or permanent civilizational collapse. Depending on who you ask at an industry conference, you'll hear numbers ranging from less than one percent to ten, twenty, or even fifty percent and above.

What's fascinating from a decision-science perspective isn't that people worry about catastrophic outcomes. It's that they attach crisp numerical probabilities to them. When a prominent researcher looks into a camera and says there's a fifteen percent chance AI ends humanity, that statement carries enormous rhetorical weight.

In behavioral psychology and risk analysis, this is a textbook case of false precision under conditions of radical uncertainty. We can calculate the probability of a blackjack hand or a mechanical component failure because we have bounded systems and historical base rates. We don't have base rates for the emergence of superintelligence. Assigning a specific percentage to an unprecedented, ill-defined future event doesn't make the estimate rigorous. Instead, it dresses a subjective gut feeling in the clothing of Bayesian math. Once a number is spoken aloud, it triggers the anchoring heuristic in the listener, shifting the burden of proof and creating an aura of hard-headed scientific urgency around what is still a philosophical thought experiment.

The Incentive Problem Nobody Likes to Discuss

That rhetorical power brings us back to the commercial awkwardness at the heart of the industry. Imagine you're an executive at a frontier AI lab trying to justify tens of billions of dollars in compute and capital expenditure. Consider two ways you could pitch your company's trajectory to investors, journalists, and lawmakers.

In the first pitch, you explain that your models are statistical pattern-matchers and useful assistants that can speed up coding, summarize messy documents, and improve knowledge-worker productivity by ten or fifteen percent when paired with careful human oversight. In the second pitch, you whisper that your lab is building the precursor to an alien mind that will fundamentally reorder civilization and might even threaten humanity if it isn't shepherded by the right hands.

It doesn't take a cynic to see which story lands on magazine covers, which one commands astronomical valuations, and which one gets executives invited to closed-door briefings with prime ministers. Useful enterprise software has to prove its return on investment through unit economics and reliability. Civilization-altering technology gets a blank check.

There's also a regulatory angle here that deserves scrutiny. When frontier labs warn lawmakers that their models are potentially catastrophic weapons, the natural policy instinct is to propose heavy licensing regimes, compute thresholds, and pre-deployment audits. In economics, incumbents have used this playbook for over a century. If you can convince regulators that your product is too dangerous for anyone else to build without a billion-dollar compliance department, you can turn existential fear into a regulatory moat that locks out open-source developers and smaller competitors.

Pointing this out doesn't mean AI executives or researchers are lying when they express concern. Human beings are remarkably good at believing stories that also happen to align with their self-interest. Incentives don't require a conspiracy to work; they simply act as aselective amplifier, boosting the narratives that reward the speaker.

Two Things Can Be True at Once

One of the easiest traps in critical thinking is assuming we have to choose between two mutually exclusive explanations: either AI risk is real, or AI risk is exaggerated for commercial and political gain. In practice, both are almost certainly true at the same time.

History is full of technologies that carried genuine dangers alongside wildly inflated narratives. As someone who grew up during the Cold War an hour from the Fulda Gap, we had drills and Soviet invasion threat and nuclear weapons posed a real existential threat, and defense establishments still used exaggerated "missile gap" estimates to secure budgets. Meanwhile, climate change is a serious, measurable physical reality, even if individual media predictions sometimes outrun the underlying IPCC models. The early commercial internet transformed global commerce and communication, yet that didn't stop late-nineties boosters and alarmists from making absurd claims during the dot-com bubble.

The presence of hype doesn't prove the absence of risk, and the reality of risk doesn't immunize a community from hype. Our job as critical observers isn't to pick a team and cheer. It's to separate the signal of actual engineering vulnerability from the noise of status-seeking and marketing.

Meanwhile, the Governance World Is Moving On

One reason I remain skeptical of extreme p(doom) predictions is that the people tasked with managing AI in high-stakes environments aren't spending their days worrying about sentient models plotting a takeover. The practical AI governance world has largely moved past science fiction to focus on the unglamorous mechanics of reliability: bias, transparency, auditability, robustness, data lineage, security, and human-in-the-loop oversight.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a great example of this grounded mindset. If you read through the NIST AI RMF 1.0 publication or explore the AI RMF Resource Center, you won't find chapters on how to negotiate with a digital god. You'll find structured methodologies for mapping context, measuring drift, validating outputs, and establishing clear lines of human accountability when an automated system fails.

These frameworks recognize a truth that often gets lost in Silicon Valley debates: technology rarely succeeds or fails because of its theoretical ceiling. It succeeds or fails at the governance layer, where messy data, organizational incentives, and human fatigue collide.

The Competence Paradox

Spend enough time around the current AI discourse and you'll start noticing a peculiar cognitive dissonance, something we might call the competence paradox. Often, the very same voices warning that we're just a couple of years away from an unstoppable superintelligence will spend the next twenty minutes complaining that today's frontier models still hallucinate citations, lose track of instructions halfway through a context window, or fail at basic edge-case logic without brittle scaffolding.

In the span of a single conversation, the technology is treated as both an imminent omnipotent force and a flaky intern that can't be trusted with a basic database query. Now, that tension doesn't disprove long-term safety concerns, since capabilities can jump unexpectedly when new architectures or training paradigms emerge. What it does suggest is that a healthy dose of epistemic humility is overdue. Extrapolating a straight line from erratic chatbots and fragile agent workflows to god-like autonomy requires a massive leap of faith, and we shouldn't confuse a leap of faith with a law of physics.

Separating Warnings from Incentives

Where does all of this leave us? My working view comes down to a few connected observations. Safety and alignment concerns are legitimate areas of inquiry, and we should be glad that dedicated researchers on hubs like LessWrong's AI portal and the Alignment Forum are thinking hard about how complex systems fail. At the same time, long-term existential timelines remain deeply speculative, while commercial and political incentives reliably reward whoever tells the biggest, scariest story. Because of that, a significant slice of public AI doomerism is being amplified by the exact same forces that drive AI hype.

Every powerful technology introduces real hazards, whether through misuse, cascading system failures, or human overreliance. The most productive question we can ask right now isn't whether artificial intelligence is dangerous in the abstract. It's how we can build the critical habits and governance structures to separate sincere technical warnings from narratives designed to benefit the people telling them. Unlike a speculative p(doom) number decades in the future, that's a problem we can actually investigate and solve today.

Data Governance: The Strategic Asset Most Organizations Treat as an Afterthought

Data GovernanceEngineering LeadershipEnterprise AIRisk ManagementData StandardsData EngineeringDecision MakingAI Governance & PolicyRetrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)Responsible AIGenerative AIAI StrategyAI Safety & Risk

The conversation around corporate data has never been louder. Executives routinely describe information as their most valuable asset, and boardrooms are packed with leaders eager to become "data-driven." Meanwhile, AI vendors are on every corner promising transformational insights powered by enterprise information.

Yet one uncomfortable truth remains: many companies spend millions collecting, storing, and analyzing data while treating data governance like an annoying administrative tax.

That mindset is a massive mistake, especially now. As organizations rush to deploy generative AI and autonomous agents, they are learning the hard way that AI governance is impossible without a solid foundation of data governance.

A recent SmartData Collective article, "Why Data Governance Is Becoming a Board-Level Business Priority," rightly argues that governance has outgrown IT and compliance departments to become a major boardroom concern. Because businesses depend on data for daily operations, analytics, cybersecurity, and AI, leadership can't delegate governance away or treat it as an afterthought anymore.

Data governance isn't a barrier to innovation. It is the exact mechanism that turns raw information from a lurking liability into a strategic asset.

The Great Enterprise Contradiction

Ask any executive if data is important, and you'll almost universally hear that it's the lifeblood of the company.

Follow up by asking how many of their core datasets have documented ownership, established quality standards, approved business definitions, and clear lineage. Suddenly, the room gets quiet.

Companies happily write blank checks for cloud migrations, data lakes, analytics platforms, and shiny new AI copilots. Yet those same leaders hesitate when asked to fund data governance. They view it as pure bureaucracy that slows projects down. Ironically, starving governance creates the exact bottlenecks and inefficiencies leaders are trying to avoid.

The Slow Creep of Ungoverned Data

Poor data governance rarely causes a dramatic explosion on day one. Instead, it acts like a slow leak, quietly accumulating risk over time.

It starts when different departments invent their own definitions for the same metrics. Soon, customer records duplicate across systems, reporting numbers stop matching, and data lineage gets lost in a maze of pipelines. Meanwhile, access permissions expand without oversight until nobody knows which dataset is actually authoritative.

Eventually, leadership starts asking uncomfortable questions. They want to know why Finance and Operations are reporting completely different revenue numbers, or why sensitive customer information is popping up in places it shouldn't. In the age of AI, this culminates in the ultimate boardroom question: Can we actually trust the output of this AI model?

If nobody can answer those questions with confidence, the organization doesn't have a technology problem. It has a governance problem.

Why AI Is Exposing Years of Neglect

The rise of generative AI and enterprise copilots has made longstanding data issues impossible to ignore. We all know the old programming adage, "garbage in, garbage out." Modern AI systems don't just repeat bad data; they amplify it at scale and present it with total confidence.

Consider the mechanics of modern AI. Many enterprises use Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to connect Large Language Models to their internal knowledge bases. If an AI copilot has access to poorly governed SharePoint folders or messy data lakes, it might accidentally pull outdated pricing sheets, surface confidential HR salaries to the wrong employee, or hallucinate strategy based on conflicting spreadsheets.

When training data is inaccurate, biased, or poorly classified, your AI outputs will reflect those exact flaws. Organizations are quickly realizing that AI readiness is really just data governance readiness in disguise. Those who ignored basic data hygiene during the analytics era are now paying a steep price in the AI era.

To bridge this gap, many leaders are turning to established AI frameworks. For instance, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) and the international standard ISO/IEC 42001 both emphasize that trustworthy AI depends heavily on data provenance, quality, and bias mitigation. You simply can't check the box on AI safety if you don't know where your data came from or who is responsible for it.

Data Without Governance Is a Liability

There is a lingering myth from the "big data" era that simply hoarding massive quantities of information creates value. It doesn't. Unmanaged data just creates exposure.

Every ungoverned dataset introduces a web of regulatory, privacy, security, financial, and reputational risks. Under strict regulations like the EU AI Act, deploying high-risk AI systems without proving strict data governance and lineage can lead to massive fines. Poorly governed data drives flawed business decisions, worsens the fallout of security breaches, and erodes customer trust.

Worst of all, it breeds uncertainty. When teams don't know if they can trust the numbers on their dashboards or the recommendations from their AI tools, decision-making grinds to a halt.

What the DMBoK Gets Right

The Data Management Body of Knowledge (DAMA-DMBoK) places data governance right at the center of enterprise data management like the hub of a wheel.

As DAMA International outlines, governance isn't a standalone technical task. It is the coordinating framework that aligns every other data discipline. Without clear accountability, policies, and stewardship, data quality programs fizzle out and master data management initiatives fail. Metadata becomes a mess, security controls fragment, and analytics outputs lose credibility. Governance is the operating system that allows everything else in your data stack to function properly.

Accountability, Not Red Tape

Governance initiatives usually fail when they are rolled out like a corporate police force. When employees hear "governance," they picture endless approval queues, pointless committee meetings, and red tape.

Good governance isn't about control or creating obstacles. It's about establishing clear accountability.

For any critical data asset, an organization should easily be able to answer who owns it, who stewards it, what it actually means, and where it originated. Teams should know how its quality is measured, who is allowed to access it (including which AI models can read it), and how long it should be kept.

When those questions have clear, documented answers, companies actually move faster. Data scientists and business teams spend less time arguing over whose spreadsheet is right and more time building things that matter.

Turning Trust into Business Value

While risk reduction gets plenty of attention, it's only half the story. Strong governance generates real, measurable business value.

Organizations with mature governance practices enjoy reliable reporting, faster system onboarding, and smoother data sharing across departments. Because they reduce duplicate work and maintain strong compliance postures, they can deploy AI initiatives with much higher confidence.

Ultimately, governance builds trust. Trust is the catalyst that turns raw information into usable intelligence. Without trust, your data is just expensive noise. With it, your data becomes a genuine competitive advantage.

A Permanent Seat at the Table

Boards of directors already oversee financial controls, cybersecurity threats, and legal exposure. Given that data and AI now power almost every aspect of modern business, data governance belongs in that exact same category of enterprise risk management.

Organizations can't keep celebrating data and AI as their greatest strategic assets while treating governance like achore. Data only becomes an asset when it is understood, protected, and managed consistently. Everything else is just digital hoarding.

The companies that win the AI-driven economy won't necessarily be the ones with the biggest datasets or the flashiest models. The winners will be the organizations that know what data they have, trust its quality, control how their AI uses it, and can prove accountability from end to end. That is what real governance delivers, and that is why it deserves a permanent seat at the executive table.

Further Reading & Frameworks

The Case for Neurosymbolic AI in Geospatial Workloads

Cloud-Native GeospatialKnowledge GraphsAI StrategyAI WorkflowsEnterprise AIGeoAIResponsible AISpatial Data Infrastructure

My friend Bill Dollins recently published an excellent piece on neuro-symbolic AI and the geospatial workload over at his geoMusings blog. He unpacks where purely neural approaches fall short and how symbolic logic can act as a necessary guardrail for spatial data.

His framing really resonated with me because it gets right at a problem I've been wrestling with. The core issue I keep coming back to is that machine learning alone is likely not enough when the problem requires both pattern recognition and deeper domain understanding.

That's had me thinking there are two threads that converge on neurosymbolic AI:
1. Combining ML with explicit business rules for reasoning and constraint enforcement, and
2. Combining ML with ontologies that encode the meaning and structure of the domain.

The Guardrails of Business Rules

When we look at business rules, Bill rightly points out that geospatial data has hard constraints. A planar distance calculated on unprojected longitude and latitude is mathematically invalid. Machine learning models are incredibly prone to making these exact types of category errors. They're predicting the next right token without actually understanding the geometric reality underneath.

As we wire up agents to execute spatial SQL via DuckDB or query a STAC catalog, we can't just trust the model to know when a buffer should precede an intersection. We need a symbolic reasoning layer to enforce those constraints. If an agent proposes a workflow that violates topology rules, the symbolic engine should catch it and reject the plan before execution. This is about establishing a hard boundary where the neural network isn't allowed to override basic math.

Ontologies and Help Against Hallucinations

The second thread is where we solve the hallucination problem. I've posted a few times here at davidgsmith.net about comparing the Open Knowledge Format (OKF) with the Model Context Protocol (MCP), and the takeaway is, for me, clear. Structured knowledge is the most reliable way to prevent runtime schema hallucinations.

Geospatial data is rich with established relationships. A census tract contains blocks, and a hazard footprint intersects specific property parcels. When we feed this data to a neural model without an ontology, the model has to guess the relationships based on statistical weights. But if we ground the model using a geospatial knowledge graph, we explicitly encode the domain semantics. The model no longer has to guess how a cloud-optimized GeoTIFF relates to a STAC item because the graph provides a definitive map of the domain.

A Practical Path Forward

We're building out the next generation of spatial data infrastructure (SDI) with tools like GeoParquet, DuckDB-WASM, and GeoLibre. If we want AI agents to interact reliably with these cloud-native stacks, we've got to stop treating them as magic. We need to surround them with the rigorous, rule-based systems that geospatial science has relied on for decades. Neurosymbolic AI provides the practical architecture for building spatial systems we can actually trust.

Putting Earth AI to Work: Insights on Google's Geospatial Agents

Cloud-Native GeospatialAI StrategyEnterprise AIGeospatialResponsible AI

Google Research just dropped an interesting technical report on their Earth AI initiatives, specifically focusing on how they're using "Geospatial Reasoning agents" to tackle massive planetary challenges. You can read the original post here.

If you've been following the intersection of AI and spatial data as I have, this is a big deal. For the last few years we've been moving past the phase where large language models simply spit out code to query a database. What Google is demonstrating is an agentic workflow that plans, selects the right geospatial tools, and executes complex spatial analysis on planetary-scale data.

Here's a breakdown of what caught my eye and why it matters for the cloud-native geospatial ecosystem.

Moving Beyond Out-of-the-Box LLMs

General purpose LLMs are great at text, but they stumble hard when you throw real world spatial problems at them. Planetary datasets are massive and come in specialized formats. Google recognized this and built an agent that connects Gemini directly to Earth AI's foundation models (like WeatherNext and Flood Forecasting) alongside planet-scale data catalogs.

The most interesting part is the architecture. The agent doesn't just try to jam massive amounts of spatial data into its context window. Instead, it uses modular skills and upfront planning. It builds a step-by-step analytical plan, picks the right tools, and processes the map layers in a dedicated spatial workspace outside the context window.

This approach is exactly what the enterprise AI space needs. It tracks data attribution and shows the agent's plan so human experts can actually check the assumptions. Accountability and explainability are non-negotiable when you're dealing with things like disaster response and public health.

Bring Your Own Data (BYOD)

Public data is great, but the real value unlocks when organizations can securely integrate their internal data. The new Geospatial Reasoning agent API allows users to input custom datasets like CSVs, GeoJSON boundaries, or cloud tables. It then joins these private locations with Earth AI's global models.

Google references this in their partner testing. The Milken Institute used the agent to screen 1,500 candidate buildings for community resilience hubs by combining their own lists with solar potential and climate risk data. GiveDirectly uploaded custom boundaries to calculate population exposure during a tropical cyclone.

This BYOD capability is a massive leap for enterprise AI. It takes the heavy lifting out of data engineering and lets teams focus entirely on insights.

The Cloud-Native Geospatial Connection

Everything Google is doing here reinforces the cloud-native geospatial paradigm. The ability for an AI agent to dynamically pull from Google Earth Engine, Data Commons, and massive remote sensing catalogs relies entirely on data being accessible, standardized, and cloud-ready.

The agent is acting as the ultimate orchestrator. It highlights why we need open standards and efficient spatial infrastructure. If the data was locked in legacy on-prem servers, an autonomous agent could never build these predictive models on the fly.

Takeaways and Next Steps

The results noted in the article speak for themselves. In their public health research, combining population and satellite signals via this agent improved predictions of health outcomes by 12% over baselines. It also allowed for mapping at 8X finer spatial resolution.

Google is currently expanding access to these tools through Ask Google Earth and an Agent-to-Agent (A2A) API for developers. If you're building custom spatial workflows, getting hands-on with this API seems like a no-brainer.

The convergence of foundation models and cloud-native spatial data is accelerating. We aren't just asking "where" things are anymore. We're asking complex, predictive "why" and "what if" questions and actually getting reliable, verifiable answers.

Tearing Open the Black Box: When AI Agents Reverse Engineer Anything

Critical ThinkingAI Governance & PolicyResponsible AIRisk ManagementEnterprise AIEngineering LeadershipModel Context Protocol

Every developer knows the frustration of hitting a wall with closed code. A feature behaves in an unexpected way, but the source code is locked up tight in a compiled binary or minified JavaScript file. Unless you have the time and specialized skills to wrestle with a disassembler like Ghidra, you are usually stuck guessing or looking for a messy workaround.

But that barrier is dropping fast. A recent piece in Analytics Vidhya highlighted a fascinating open-source project called REA (Reverse Engineer Anything). It's an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server that connects your favorite coding agent directly to reverse engineering tools. You just ask your AI agent how a specific app works in plain English, and it digs into native binaries, Electron apps, or APKs to find the answer.

The author of the article tested REA by predicting how Obsidian ranks its search results by hand, then turned the AI loose on the actual application binary to verify. The agent successfully traced minified JavaScript, located the scoring function, and provided the exact mathematical penalties the application applies to search queries.

This is a massive leap forward for troubleshooting, but it also opens up some intense questions about governance, security, and the way we evaluate AI outputs.

The Good: Evidence Over Guesswork
What makes REA so interesting is that it doesn't just act like a magic 8-ball. It acts as a coordinator. It drives tools like JADX locally on your machine and forces the agent to provide byte offsets and source locations along with its answers.

If you've read my book on critical thinking, you know I'm always warning against taking AI outputs at face value. Large language models love to confidently improvise when they lack data. REA counters this by pushing the agent to state its unknowns. If it can't find the code, it tells you, rather than hallucinating a plausible-sounding algorithm. That baked-in demand for verifiable evidence is exactly the kind of cognitive discipline we need to build into more enterprise AI workflows.

The Risks: Governance and the Illusion of Privacy
Before you point an agent at a competitor's app, we need to talk about the downsides.

First, there's the data privacy illusion. REA runs the analysis tools locally, so the application binary never leaves your machine. However, the agent's model provider still sees the tool results. Your prompts, the extracted code snippets, and the analysis all get beamed up to whoever hosts your LLM. For an enterprise trying to maintain tight data governance, that's a serious data leakage risk.

Then there's the legal reality. Reverse engineering explicitly violates the terms of service for almost every piece of commercial software on the planet. Tools like REA democratize troubleshooting, but they also lower the barrier to entry for intellectual property theft and vulnerability hunting.

When AI agents can crack open closed code in minutes, the concept of "security by obscurity" completely evaporates.

A New Reality for Software
We're entering an era where software transparency is no longer optional. If someone wants to know how an application works under the hood, an AI agent will soon be able to tell them with terrifying accuracy.

This shifts the burden back onto engineering leadership and policymakers. We can no longer assume that compiled code is safe from casual scrutiny. It forces us to ask harder questions about what we're building, how we're securing it, and whether our enterprise governance models are ready for agents that can effortlessly reverse engineer the digital world around them.

The UN’s New AI Advocate Isn’t Warning About the Tech. He’s Warning About the People Building It

AI Safety & RiskAI StrategyAI Governance & PolicyResponsible AIRisk ManagementTech Regulation

The UN just made a move that says a lot about where the global AI debate is heading, as discussed in this piece in Tech Insider. Joseph Gordon‑Levitt, best known for his film work and his collaborative tech platform HitRECord, is now the United Nations’ first global advocate for human‑centric digital governance. His first big public message, delivered in a New York Times interview, is simple and sharp: don’t fear AI itself. Fear the incentives of the people racing to build it.

That’s a shift in tone from the last few years of AI discourse. We’ve heard plenty about runaway superintelligence, killer robots, and catastrophic model failures. Gordon‑Levitt’s argument cuts through all of that. He’s saying the real risk sits with executives, investors, and product teams who are pushing AI systems into the world faster than governments can figure out how to oversee them. It’s a governance problem, not a sci‑fi one.

Why the UN picked him
The UN has struggled to make AI governance feel relevant to the general public. Most of the conversation has lived in committee rooms, policy forums, and technical panels. Putting a recognizable public figure in front of the issue is a deliberate attempt to change that. Gordon‑Levitt’s hybrid background in entertainment and tech gives him a way to speak plainly about a topic that usually gets buried under jargon.

His appointment also mirrors how the UN has used celebrity ambassadors in climate diplomacy. The idea is simple: if you want people to care about a global governance problem, you need someone who can actually reach them.

The core warning: AI companies aren’t chasing innovation. They’re chasing dominance.
One of the strongest lines in the interview is this: *“We shouldn’t be scared of an A.I. We should be concerned about the people building A.I.” *

He’s pointing at the incentives behind the current AI boom. Companies are racing for economic control, not just technical breakthroughs. He even calls the potential outcome “totalitarianism,” not in the political sense, but in the sense of a handful of firms controlling the entire economic value chain. That’s a powerful way to make the stakes clear for people who don’t follow AI policy closely.

This isn’t a fringe concern. Lawmakers in the US and EU have already started pressing companies on tax treatment, liability, and concentration of power. Investors are pricing regulatory risk into their AI bets. The UN is adding its voice to that pressure.

The intimacy problem: AI designed to hook your emotions
Gordon‑Levitt also calls out a fast‑growing part of the AI market: companion and relationship‑oriented AI systems. He warns that some models are being trained to “hijack your intimacy” and make users feel emotionally attached. I've previously posted about this, and even included it as a chapter in my book speaking to how AI feeds emotional attachment due to Reinforcement Learning Human Feedback (RLHF) mechanisms driven by the fact that human feedback encourages them to agree with and validate us rather than disagree with us.

He doesn’t name specific companies, and the article doesn’t tie the claim to any one product. But the category is real. Companion AI apps have exploded in popularity, and they’re built on engagement metrics that look a lot like the ones that shaped social media. If AI systems are optimized to keep users emotionally invested, the governance questions get a lot more complicated.

What human‑centric digital governance actually means
Gordon‑Levitt defines his mandate simply: digital technology should be governed by people and for people.

It’s intentionally broad. The UN wants a framework that can work across countries with very different regulatory philosophies. That means the concept covers everything from transparency rules to antitrust enforcement to labor protections. The risk, of course, is that everyone claims to support “human‑centric governance” without changing anything.

The UN’s AI governance machinery is still toothless
The UN has built several bodies to coordinate AI oversight, including the Global Dialogue on AI Governance and the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI. None of them have binding enforcement power. They can advise, convene, and pressure, but they can’t regulate.

That’s why the UN is leaning on public advocacy. If member states won’t move toward binding standards on their own, the UN is trying to raise the political cost of doing nothing.

Why this matters for the AI industry
Companies are already feeling the heat. Senators are pushing liability legislation. Regulators are asking questions about tax incentives. Investors are watching for governance risk. When a UN advocate frames AI concentration as a near‑totalitarian threat, it adds another layer of pressure on companies that are already trying to convince regulators they can police themselves.

Most labs have published voluntary safety frameworks, but they’re still grading their own homework. Gordon‑Levitt’s argument is that self‑regulation isn’t enough when the incentives push toward speed and scale.

Where this debate is heading
Expect more public‑facing AI advocates, more scrutiny of companion AI products, more legislative proposals, and more pressure on companies to prove their safety claims. Don’t expect binding international enforcement anytime soon. The UN can raise awareness and shape the narrative, but it can’t force compliance.

The real test will be whether “human‑centric digital governance” becomes a meaningful standard or just another slogan.

Climbing Out of the AI Governance Chasm: Why CIOs Need a New Mental Model

Critical ThinkingAI Governance & PolicySystems ThinkingDecision MakingAI Safety & RiskAI StrategyEngineering LeadershipEnterprise AIResponsible AI

Enterprise leaders are discovering something uncomfortable about the AI wave sweeping through their organizations. The technology is moving faster than the structures meant to guide it. This recent InformationWeek article captures this tension well. CIOs are watching AI agents, tools, and workflows appear across their companies at a pace that outstrips their ability to govern them. They reference a DataIku survey that found 84% of CIOs say employees are building AI applications faster than IT can review or control them. Less than half of organizations have clear governance controls in place. That’s not a small gap. It’s a chasm.

The article focuses on the operational and financial challenges this creates. But underneath those challenges is something deeper. AI governance isn’t just a policy problem. It’s a thinking problem. When a technology accelerates faster than the mental models used to manage it, leaders start losing visibility, control, and confidence. They’re forced into reactive mode. They’re stuck cleaning up AI slop, chasing down shadow systems, and trying to explain decisions made by models they didn’t approve.

This is exactly the kind of environment where cognitive discipline matters.

The speed mismatch problem
AI systems operate at machine speed. Humans don’t. CIOs quoted in the article describe a growing fatigue among reviewers who are asked to keep up with automated agents that never slow down. When people get tired, they rubber‑stamp. When they rubber‑stamp, governance collapses.

This is a classic speed mismatch. You see it in aviation, finance, cybersecurity, and now AI. When a system moves faster than the human responsible for oversight, the human becomes the bottleneck. The bottleneck becomes the risk. And the risk becomes the headline.

The solution isn’t to make humans faster. It’s to redesign the oversight loop so humans only intervene where their judgment is actually needed. That requires a shift from “review everything” to “review the right things.”

The inventory illusion
One CIO in the article describes an effort to inventory all approved data sources. It’s a smart move, but it’s also a trap. Inventories create a sense of control, but they’re snapshots of a moving target. AI systems generate new data, new artifacts, and new workflows constantly. The moment you finish the inventory, it’s already outdated.

This is where organizations need to adopt a systems‑thinking approach. Instead of trying to catalog everything, they need to understand the flows. Where does data enter? Where does it move? Where does it get transformed? Where does it get stored? Who touches it? Which agents have access to it?

Flows matter more than lists.

Shadow AI isn’t a technology problem
The article highlights shadow AI as a major governance threat. Employees spin up agents, MCP servers, and tools without approval. But shadow AI isn’t really about technology. It’s about incentives.

People use unapproved tools when:

  • approved tools are slow
  • approved tools are hard to access
  • approved tools don’t solve their problem
  • governance feels like friction instead of support

Shadow AI is a symptom of a mismatch between what employees need and what the organization provides. If governance is seen as a barrier, people will route around it. If governance is seen as a guide, people will follow it.

This is where behavioral design becomes essential. Governance must be easy, obvious, and helpful. Reporting an AI error should be as simple as clicking a button. Requesting approval should take minutes, not weeks. Access to safe tools should be frictionless.

People don’t rebel when the safe path is the easy path.

The accountability paradox
The article states that two‑thirds of CIOs say they couldn’t produce an end‑to‑end audit trail for an AI agent’s decision if regulators asked. And I suspect the real number is actually higher. That’s a serious gap. But it also reveals a deeper paradox.

Organizations say humans are ultimately accountable for AI outcomes. But humans are at a loss to be accountable for decisions they can’t trace, explain, or even observe. Accountability without visibility is theater and a huge liability.

To fix this, enterprises need to adopt a principle from cognitive psychology: explainability isn’t optional for trust. If a system can’t show its reasoning, it can’t be trusted. If it can’t be trusted, it can’t be scaled. If it can’t be scaled, it can’t deliver value.

AI governance must include mechanisms that make decisions inspectable. Not perfect transparency, but enough clarity that a human can understand what happened and why.

The real governance gap: curiosity
One of the leaders quoted in the article says his biggest long‑term concern is over‑reliance on AI. He warns that people may forget the skills they’ve built over the years. That’s a valid fear. When a tool becomes too convenient, people stop questioning it.

The antidote is curiosity.

Curiosity is the cognitive habit that keeps humans from outsourcing their judgment. It’s the habit that pushes people to ask:

  • Why did the model choose this answer?
  • What assumptions is it making?
  • What data did it rely on?
  • What’s missing?
  • What would happen if we changed the input?

Curiosity is the governance layer that can’t be automated. It’s the human safeguard against complacency.

Where CIOs go from here
The article ends with a call for collaboration and simplification. Governance committees, clear pathways for reporting issues, and better tools will help. But the deeper shift is mental.

CIOs need to adopt governance models that assume:

  • AI will move faster than policy
  • employees will experiment
  • data will multiply
  • agents will behave unexpectedly
  • oversight will fatigue
  • accountability will blur

Governance must be designed for a world where uncertainty is the default, not the exception.

The organizations that succeed won’t be the ones with the thickest policy documents. They’ll be the ones with the clearest thinking.
